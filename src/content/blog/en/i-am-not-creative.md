_It's the sentence I hear most often. Before a workshop, during it, and sometimes even after, in front of a painting the person has just finished and finds more or less successful._

**"I'm warning you, I'm terrible, I can't draw"**: that's what I hear every time someone comes to one of my workshops for the first time, often before they even have a brush in their hands.

It's said without hesitation, the way you'd announce your shoe size. **A fact, not an opinion**. And that's where it gets interesting, because it's **almost always false**.

## The word "creative" has shrunk

When you say "I'm not creative", you mean something very specific: **I can't draw**. At a stretch: I can't paint, sing, or write.

**The word has shrunk until it only means the fine arts.** Yet this week, you probably did one or several of these things:

- opened the fridge at 7 pm, saw three leftovers that didn't go together, and made dinner anyway;
- explained the same thing twice to someone, then found a third way to say it, the one that worked;
- fixed something with an object that wasn't made for that (and felt like MacGyver);
- chosen the order in which to tell a story so that it would make people laugh;
- arranged objects on a shelf until it felt just right, without being able to explain why.

None of these things requires knowing how to draw. All of them require exactly what a watercolor requires: looking at what you have, imagining what it could become, trying, adjusting.

**You're not lacking creativity**. You just call it something else: resourcefulness, organization, DIY, common sense. Those are the names we give creativity when it's useful. **The rest of the time, we call it talent**, and decide we don't have any.

## Where this sentence comes from

<img class="img-right" src="/images/waterlili-atelier-aquarelle-enfants.jpg" alt="Watercolor workshop with children in Haute-Savoie, France" />

Nobody is born thinking they're not creative. At six years old, we draw without wondering whether we know how to draw. **We do it, we show it, we move on.**

And then there's a moment. A grade, a remark, a classmate who drew better, an instruction we misunderstood. Someone said something, or nobody said anything at all, which is enough too.

**That day, you didn't stop being creative**. You stopped showing it. Then, from lack of practice, you stopped doing it. And twenty or thirty years later, there's a ready-made sentence left that you say without thinking.

Where it struck me the most was when I started running watercolor workshops at markets and events. Even though nothing about my stand suggested it, only children came up to it. And when I invited the adults with them to paint with us, their answer was almost always **"Oh no, I can't paint"**. Yet the question they ask their child is "Do you feel like painting?"

## You're confusing creating with succeeding

Here's the real knot. When you say you're not creative, you're not talking about your ability to create. **You're talking about the result**. You've already decided that if it isn't beautiful, it doesn't count.

It's a rule you apply nowhere else. You cook without being a chef. You sing in the car without knowing how to sing. You dance at a wedding. **Nobody demands that it be successful**, because everyone has understood that it's not the point.

For painting, strangely, we allow ourselves to demand a result. So we don't start.

**Creating doesn't have to be useful, profitable, or successful.** Even a failed watercolor made you spend an hour looking very closely at a flower. You had that time. It doesn't depend on the result.

## What really changes when you get back to it

It's not that you become good. It's **quieter and more useful** than that.

**You start looking again**. Spend an hour painting a lemon and you'll never see a lemon quite the same way again: you'll have noticed that the yellow is green in places, that the skin isn't smooth, that the shadow isn't gray. It doesn't stop when you put the brush down. It's what makes days feel less automatic.

<img class="img-left" src="/images/coucher-de-soleil-creatif-waterlili.webp" alt="Sunset" />
And you make something, instead of consuming something. An evening of scrolling and an evening of painting last the same amount of time. What remains afterwards isn't the same.

The most lasting thing that happened to me isn't having learned to paint. It's having learned to look. To paint a sunset as well as possible, you have to watch some in "real life", observe very closely to understand how nature manages such a beautiful transition between blue and yellow without going through green. To paint the magical atmosphere I have in mind, I have to observe how a ray of sunlight passes through the trees in a forest... And that lets me see all those small, simple details of everyday life that I used to completely miss. They now bring **a little magic and joy to my life.**

And ultimately, you don't need to know how to paint a sunset for this to happen to you too. All it takes is to have looked at something closely enough to try to recreate it.

## Where to start, without buying anything

You don't need supplies, a class, or talent. Three things you can do this week:

- **Notice five shades of the same color**. On a walk, look for five different greens. That's all. It's a complete creativity exercise, and it's free.
- **Make something with your hands, badly**. A doodle, a fold, a lopsided cake. The goal is for it not to be beautiful, to check that nobody comes to criticize you for it.
- **Don't show it to anyone.** Not yet. Creativity comes back faster when it has no audience.

If after three weeks you find yourself coming back to it on your own, that's the time to buy a twelve-euro box of paints. Not before.
